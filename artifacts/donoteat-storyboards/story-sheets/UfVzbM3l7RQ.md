## Premise and central argument

The presenter asks how Greenwood, Tulsa’s prosperous Black neighborhood, accumulated wealth, how an alleged elevator incident became a racist massacre, and why the neighborhood ultimately disappeared despite rebuilding afterward. His explanation begins with O. W. Gurley’s land purchases and unusually local circulation of money under segregation, then follows Dick Rowland, Sarah Page, the courthouse crisis, white mob organization, fire and detention. He argues that Black wealth did not insulate residents from racist power, that community self-defense was necessary despite overwhelming odds, and that Interstate 244 and Oklahoma State University’s campus inflicted more lasting spatial damage than the 1921 attack because they made formerly residential and commercial land unavailable for rebuilding. These are the presenter’s own historical and political arguments.

## Ending, payoff and explicit connections

The strongest narrative reversal is that **Greenwood rebuilt**: the presenter’s final account places its prosperity peak in the 1940s, then distinguishes recoverable burned buildings from the later permanent loss of usable neighborhood land. The campus and freeway images recur to pay off that distinction. The ending asks that communities keep their resources and the chance to rebuild, instead of receiving development schemes that displace their land uses. The Patreon commercial reprises the one-dollar, share-the-link joke, admits that sources disagree on the chronology, and ends in patron credits. The narrator names Red Vienna and the Lower Manhattan Expressway as possible future subjects but doesn’t tie this episode to any other in the collection.
