## Premise and central argument

Following Part 1's account of slum clearance and early public-housing alternatives, donoteat01 asks what happened when US housing policy increasingly routed public resources through private owners and developers. He depicts subsidy programs, redevelopment incentives, demolition, and constraints on organized labor as mechanisms that prevent housing from functioning as an anti-market public good. A fictional neighborhood dramatizes the loss of relationships behind aggregate redevelopment figures; Boston and Philadelphia then supply his concrete examples, including an unusually personal admission of participation in Philadelphia Housing Authority surveys. He ultimately advocates tenant organizing now, tenant-protected land-use reform in the medium term, and universal, rapidly expanded public housing in the long term. The historical, statistical, legal, and policy claims below are his.

## Ending and episode connections

The policy payoff refuses a choice between tenant protections and increased supply, and refuses the idea that public housing must mean isolated towers reserved for the poorest. Its final callback to Red Vienna joins the opening's Part 1 recap to an affirmative universal model. The 52nd Street project and Mikes explicitly extend earlier neighborhood-displacement stories; this installment follows their dispersal after redevelopment rather than treating demolition as an ending. The closing names Franklin as the next series installment and Killdozer as a supporter bonus; it also promises a later discussion of zoning and private housing, without naming a specific episode.

## Visual storytelling and character action

The fictional 52nd Street project is made legible through successive viewpoints, a developer logo, a mock architectural brochure, then spectacular destruction. “The Mikes” and Michelle exist **only** in the narration. Buildings, geography, an elevator photograph and the replacement neighborhood stand in for their displacement.

Boston and Philadelphia sections shift into satellite imagery, dated aerials, Street View, photographs, planning graphics, website navigation, and news screenshots. The Sharswood sequence moves from proposals and institutional headquarters to demolition, exposed party walls, and missing buildings, giving physical continuity to the account of neglect. A 2009 Oxford Street image carries the explicit qualification **“*2009, BUT I WAS THERE IN 2014 AND IT LOOKED ABOUT THE SAME”**: the photo dates from 2009, the narrator's visit from 2014.

The pension argument is unusually diagrammatic: labeled stick figures around a conference table, top hats distinguishing employers, changing employer colors, and blunt proposal/response captions. Its visual punch line is that the “dick pic” investment is illustrated with three men named Richard, not explicit photographs. The final reforms play against continued simulated fires, emergency vehicles, and damaged city blocks; the contrast is plain, though it is unclear whether individual fires are timed to particular sentences.
