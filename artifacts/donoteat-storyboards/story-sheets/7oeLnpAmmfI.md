## Premise and central argument

Following Part 1's account of slum clearance and early public-housing alternatives, donoteat01 asks what happened when US housing policy increasingly routed public resources through private owners and developers. His argument is not simply that public housing failed: he depicts subsidy programs, redevelopment incentives, demolition, and constraints on organized labor as mechanisms that prevent housing from functioning as an anti-market public good. A fictional neighborhood dramatizes the loss of relationships behind aggregate redevelopment figures; Boston and Philadelphia then supply his concrete examples, including an unusually personal admission of participation in Philadelphia Housing Authority surveys. He ultimately advocates tenant organizing now, tenant-protected land-use reform in the medium term, and universal, rapidly expanded public housing in the long term. Historical, statistical, legal, and policy assertions below are the presenter's account, not independently verified findings.
