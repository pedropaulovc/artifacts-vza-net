## Premise and central argument

While constructing a huge river-mouth fort for the fictional colonial port of Franklin, the presenter argues that the American Revolution changed the identity of the governing elite much more than it changed the economic position of poor people, enslaved people, or Indigenous peoples. The episode begins with a contemporary appeal to abolish ICE, tying deportability to workers’ inability to resist exploitation, then revisits the settlement geography established earlier in the series. The fort provides a continuous material backdrop to an account of privateering, revolutionary mobilization, restricted political participation, emancipation, land ownership, and the postwar state. A deliberately grotesque, first-person Cambodia digression satirizes apologetics for supposedly emancipatory revolutions before the presenter returns to Shays’ Rebellion and the injunction that “tyranny is tyranny, let it come from whom it may.” These historical and political claims are the presenter’s account, not independent findings of this sheet.

## Ending and series connections

The spoken ending qualifies the class-based account by naming Paine and Banneker, recommends independent reading, and makes the Boston slogan the payoff. It does not resolve the tension by choosing one ruling faction as automatically good. Episode 2 is explicitly recalled in the ICE preamble through the oppression-of-workers/mercantilism connection. The Lenape villages and Norwegian forts are explicitly revisited from earlier episodes, but their episode numbers are not supplied here. Fort Montresor advances Franklin’s fictional settlement history from an undefended mercantile port to a fortified revolutionary-era city. No later selected episode is explicitly named in the transcript.
