## Premise and central argument

While constructing a huge river-mouth fort for the fictional colonial port of Franklin, the presenter argues that the American Revolution changed the governing elite much more than the position of poor people, enslaved people or Indigenous peoples. An opening appeal to abolish ICE links deportability to workers’ inability to resist exploitation. He then revisits the earlier settlements and builds Fort Montresor while discussing privateering, revolutionary mobilization, restricted political participation, emancipation, land ownership and the postwar state. A grotesque first-person Cambodia digression satirizes excuses for supposedly emancipatory revolutions. He returns to Shays’ Rebellion and the closing injunction that “tyranny is tyranny, let it come from whom it may.”

## Ending and series connections

The spoken ending names Paine and Banneker, recommends further reading, and returns to the Boston slogan without treating either ruling faction as automatically good. The ICE preamble recalls episode 2’s discussion of worker oppression under mercantilism. Earlier Lenape villages and Norwegian forts return, though their episode numbers are not given. Fort Montresor takes Franklin from an undefended mercantile port to a fortified revolutionary-era city. No later episode is named.

The narrator’s gravelly, level delivery brings to mind a contractor patiently explaining why a home addition will cost ten times as much and take three times as long. The weary tone is an odd fit for the memes and jokes about cities, architecture and history.
