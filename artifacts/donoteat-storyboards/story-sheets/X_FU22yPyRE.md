## Premise and central argument

Building Franklin Hospital, the University of Franklin, and a temporary national capital gives the presenter a way to qualify the apparent benevolence of early public institutions. In his account of the fictional city's 1790s setting, a hospital funded by wealthy donors offers dangerous and ineffective treatment while serving those donors' interest in quarantining poor, mentally ill, and immigrant residents; a university principally trains Protestant clergy rather than providing mass vocational education; and the new federal government's universal-sounding rights are restricted in practice by race, sex, and property. The attractive institutional grounds therefore carry an argument about unequal access and control, not simply civic progress. He also seeds the later importance of education and medicine to redevelopment and promises future rants about their debt-funded modern forms. These historical interpretations are the presenter's claims, not independent verification.
